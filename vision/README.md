# The vision

**Status: founding intent and research direction, recorded 2026-10-25. Specific mechanisms remain proposals until supported and adopted.**

## What we are trying to achieve

Create a credible path toward communities in which increasing productivity makes ordinary people's lives materially better: essentials cost less, households build assets, useful work is rewarded, and people have more freedom over their time. AI, robotics, remote work, modern construction, resilient utilities, and better logistics create possibilities to investigate. The project must establish which combinations are practical, financeable, and desirable to the people involved.

The organizing idea is a **regional homestead**. People could choose different living arrangements around a shared region: homes near a center, more secluded rural acreage, or equipped seasonal RV sites. Centers could concentrate production, distribution, services, and gathering places while residents participate in the wider economy. A region could use nearby towns and hospitals rather than build every service from scratch.

## Everyday life the research should make concrete

A family might earn wages remotely, run a trade business, or work for a local enterprise. An older resident might remain independent with reliable delivery and access to care. A rural owner might opt to host a crop-production unit and receive compensation if its economics and impacts justify it. A seasonal resident might move between participating nodes. Residents could volunteer, care for family, learn, or build a business without every hour being driven by the cost of necessities.

These are cases to test. The research needs to show complete household budgets, practical access, daily burdens, and choices over time, including when a household loses income, develops care needs, or wants to leave.

## Productive enterprise and ownership

Private businesses, entrepreneurship, employee ownership, and voluntary participation are central to the founding intent. The project seeks arrangements through which residents share in actual gains from productive activity. Possibilities include direct ownership, employee shares, land-hosting payments, utility savings, a resident investment vehicle, or negotiated participation in enterprise cash flows.

The original policy discussion also explored whether public incentives could reflect verifiable business contributions: good employment, responsible resource use, useful innovation, and community investment. That remains a research track, alongside ownership and service arrangements that could be implemented without national policy reform.

The broader ambition includes people outside participating nodes sharing in technological progress. A national public investment fund or other wider mechanism was raised as a possibility. Research should distinguish those policy proposals from what a first local project could accomplish under existing institutions, and examine access to useful innovations as well as cash returns.

A data center, robotics company, manufacturer, farm, or energy project is a possible business case, not an established partner or assumed source of free money. Each needs customers, workable economics, capital, infrastructure, and an explicit agreement defining what the community receives. The model must distinguish company sales, wages, profits, taxes, resident ownership returns, and household cost reductions.

## Physical form and regional connections

Investigate rural parcels, town neighborhoods, mixed-use centers, production and logistics hubs, clean energy, communications, water and wastewater, practical food systems, and optional RV or mobile living. The brainstorm included 10+ acre parcels, quarter-acre RV sites, and access to major services within roughly 30-60 minutes. These are design ideas, not established planning standards or verified entitlements.

Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, and underutilized areas such as California City were suggested as search directions. No place is preferred on evidence yet. Compare low advertised land prices with complete development costs, sustainable water, legal access, hazard exposure, service availability, and local willingness. Include an alternative outside the original shortlist if the evidence points there.

## Build small; understand scale

Find the smallest useful first node, its first residents and customers, and a credible sequence of investment. Compare a parcel-based pilot, development near an existing town, and a larger planned settlement. Scenarios around 5,000 or 50,000 people and 30-, 50-, or 100-year horizons help investigate scale; they do not fix the optimal population or predict a century of outcomes.

Growth should follow demonstrated capacity and household benefit. The research must account for startup losses, working capital, construction lead times, new arrivals, housing price pressure, maintenance, replacement, and failure. Expansion to a network of nodes is a later hypothesis with its own coordination and mobility costs.

## The digital twin

The intended experience combines a changing bird's-eye landscape with the lives of hypothetical residents. A person should be able to vary assumptions, advance time, follow households, compare scenarios, and see why a result occurred. The visual layer must be driven by a transparent model of money, people, land, energy, water, housing, and service capacity.

Begin with an inspectable scenario model. Describe it as a digital twin only to the extent it is tied to a defined real place and validated observations. Simulated behavior helps examine possibilities; it does not establish real consent, forecast individual choices, or prove that everyone will be happy.

## The research organization

This repository is the common foundation for people and authorized AI agents acting as specialists. Department READMEs explain questions, expertise, outputs, and dependencies. Shared registers preserve assumptions, findings, decisions, disagreements, and handoffs. Synthesis reconciles department work into whole-node alternatives rather than simply assembling favorable reports.

The project should eventually be understandable enough for outside contributors, prospective residents, domain professionals, and potential supporters to evaluate. Fundraising, partnerships, publication, and implementation each need their own concrete proposal and authorization.

## The test of progress

Can we explain a realistic first node with a specific place, legal path, resource budget, capital plan, customers, household outcomes, and protections for individual choice? Can another person reproduce the important calculations and identify what would cause the plan to fail?
